## Introduction: Why Stories Need Struggle

Every story worth telling is built on conflict. Without it, there is no plot, no tension, no reason for the reader to keep turning pages. A story in which nothing goes wrong, no one faces a challenge, and everything works out perfectly is not a story — it is a wish.

**Conflict** is the struggle between opposing forces that drives the action of a narrative. It is the engine of plot, the crucible of character, and the source of meaning in literature. Understanding conflict is essential for understanding how stories work — and why they matter.

This guide explains conflict in depth: the major types, how they function, how to identify them, and how they shape meaning across different genres and works.

---

## What Is Conflict?

**Conflict** is the central tension or struggle in a narrative. It arises when a character's goals, desires, or values are opposed by some force — another character, society, nature, the character's own inner demons, fate, or technology.

Conflict is not the same as **plot**, though they are closely related. Plot is the sequence of events; conflict is the tension that gives those events meaning. A plot summary tells you what happens; an analysis of conflict explains why it matters and what it reveals about human experience.

### Internal vs. External Conflict

**External conflict** occurs when a character struggles against an outside force. This may be another character, a social system, a natural force, or a supernatural power.

**Internal conflict** occurs within the character's own mind — a struggle between opposing desires, values, beliefs, or emotions.

The most powerful literature combines both. Hamlet faces external conflict (Claudius, the corrupt court) and internal conflict (his own indecision, his philosophical doubts). The interaction between external and internal conflict is what makes the play so endlessly rich.

## The Major Types of Conflict

### 1. Man vs. Man (Character vs. Character)

This is the most common and most immediately dramatic form of conflict. One character's goals are opposed by another character's goals.

**Example**: In *Othello*, the conflict between Iago and Othello — Iago's scheming against Othello's trust — drives the entire tragedy. Iago's machinations are the external force that exploits Othello's internal vulnerabilities.

**Example**: In *Harry Potter*, Harry's conflict with Voldemort is the central external conflict of the series. But the conflict is also internal — Harry must choose between self-sacrifice and self-preservation, between the values Dumbledore taught him and the darkness he shares with Voldemort.

**Example**: In *The Great Gatsby*, the conflict between Gatsby and Tom Buchanan is not just about Daisy — it is about the empty materialism of the wealthy elite vs. Gatsby's romantic idealism.

### 2. Man vs. Self (Character vs. Self)

This is the most psychologically complex form of conflict. The character struggles against their own flaws, desires, fears, or contradictions.

**Example**: Hamlet's indecision is the classic example of internal conflict. He knows what he should do (avenge his father's murder) but cannot bring himself to do it. His conflict is between action and inaction, between moral certainty and philosophical doubt.

**Example**: In Fyodor Dostoevsky's *Crime and Punishment*, Raskolnikov's internal conflict — between his belief that he is above moral law and his inescapable guilt — is the novel's driving force.

**Example**: In F. Scott Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby*, Gatsby's internal conflict is between his idealized vision of Daisy and the reality of who she is. His inability to see Daisy clearly is both his greatest strength and his fatal flaw.

### 3. Man vs. Society (Character vs. Social Institutions)

The character struggles against the norms, laws, or expectations of their society. This type of conflict often explores themes of justice, freedom, conformity, and rebellion.

**Example**: In George Orwell's *1984*, Winston Smith's conflict with the Party is the central struggle of the novel. His desire for truth, individuality, and love is opposed by a totalitarian system that seeks to eliminate all three.

**Example**: In Harper Lee's *To Kill a Mockingbird*, Atticus Finch's defense of Tom Robinson pits him against the racist social structures of the American South in the 1930s.

**Example**: In Margaret Atwood's *The Handmaid's Tale*, Offred's struggle against the theocratic regime of Gilead is both a fight for survival and a fight for identity.

### 4. Man vs. Nature (Character vs. Natural Forces)

The character struggles against the natural world — weather, animals, disease, the indifferent forces of the physical universe.

**Example**: In Ernest Hemingway's *The Old Man and the Sea*, Santiago's battle with the marlin is a conflict between human will and the power of nature. The sea is both adversary and companion, beautiful and destructive.

**Example**: In Jack London's *To Build a Fire*, a man's struggle against the brutal cold of the Yukon is a conflict between human arrogance and nature's indifference.

**Example**: In *The Lord of the Rings*, the characters face not only Sauron but the physical challenges of the landscape — the mountains, the marshes, the weather — that test their endurance and determination.

### 5. Man vs. Fate/God (Character vs. Destiny)

The character struggles against a predetermined destiny, divine will, or the seemingly inevitable course of events.

**Example**: In Sophocles' *Oedipus Rex*, Oedipus attempts to flee the prophecy that he will kill his father and marry his mother — and his very attempts to escape fulfill the prophecy. The conflict between human free will and divine fate is the play's central tension.

**Example**: In Thomas Hardy's novels, characters struggle against social circumstances and a seemingly indifferent universe. In *Tess of the d'Urbervilles*, Tess's tragic fate seems predetermined by forces beyond her control — social class, gender, and what Hardy calls "the President of the Immortals" (his term for the indifferent forces that govern human existence).

### 6. Man vs. Technology (Character vs. Human Creation)

A relatively modern form of conflict in which the character struggles against technology — artificial intelligence, machines, systems of control — that has turned against its creators.

**Example**: In Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*, Victor Frankenstein's creation turns against him, forcing him to confront the consequences of his ambition. The novel asks: what happens when human creation exceeds human wisdom?

**Example**: In Kazuo Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go*, the characters' conflict is not against technology itself but against the social system that created them — a system that has reduced them to biological resources.

**Example**: In *Brave New World* by Aldous Huxley, the conflict is between the individual human spirit and a technological society that has engineered away suffering, passion, and freedom.

## Levels of Conflict

Most literary works operate on multiple levels of conflict simultaneously:

**Primary conflict**: The central struggle that drives the plot. In *Macbeth*, the primary conflict is Macbeth's ambition vs. his moral conscience (internal) and Macbeth vs. those who would stop him (external).

**Secondary conflicts**: Additional tensions that enrich the narrative. In *Macbeth*, Lady Macbeth's own guilt is a secondary internal conflict. The political conflict between Macbeth and Malcolm is a secondary external conflict.

**Background conflicts**: Larger social, political, or cosmic tensions that provide context. In *Macbeth*, the background conflict is between order and chaos, between legitimate kingship and tyranny.

The interplay between these levels of conflict creates the complexity and depth that distinguish great literature from simple storytelling.

## How Conflict Functions in a Narrative

### Conflict Drives Plot

Plot is the sequence of events that results from conflict. Without conflict, there is no plot — only a series of unrelated events. The classic plot structure — exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution — is essentially a map of how conflict develops and is resolved.

### Conflict Reveals Character

A character's response to conflict reveals their true nature. It is easy to be brave when nothing is at stake. Conflict tests characters, exposes their weaknesses, and forces them to make choices that define who they are.

**Example**: In *To Kill a Mockingbird*, Atticus Finch's decision to defend Tom Robinson — knowing he will likely lose and may face social ostracism — reveals his moral courage more powerfully than any description could.

### Conflict Creates Theme

The nature of a work's central conflict often expresses its central theme. A story about a character struggling against an unjust society has themes of justice and freedom. A story about a character struggling with their own ambition has themes of power and morality.

### Conflict Engages the Reader

Conflict creates suspense, tension, and emotional investment. The reader wants to know: Who will win? What will the character choose? How will the struggle resolve? These questions keep the reader engaged and invested in the outcome.

## Conflict Across Genres

### In Poetry

Poetic conflict is often compressed and internal. A poem may capture a single moment of tension — between desire and restraint, between memory and present reality, between life and death.

**Example**: In Gerard Manley Hopkins' "Carrion Comfort," the conflict is internal — the speaker struggles against despair and the temptation to give up: "Not, I'll not, carrion comfort, Despair, not feast on thee."

**Example**: In W.B. Yeats' "The Second Coming," the conflict is cosmic — the established order is collapsing, and a new, terrifying era is being born: "Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold."

### In Drama

Dramatic conflict must be immediate and visceral. The audience watches the conflict unfold in real time, creating a sense of urgency and emotional intensity.

**Example**: In Arthur Miller's *Death of a Salesman*, the conflict is both external (Willy vs. his employer, vs. his sons) and internal (Willy vs. his own delusions). The claustrophobic setting — the Loman house — concentrates the conflict into an inescapable pressure cooker.

**Example**: In Shakespeare's *King Lear*, the conflict operates on multiple levels: Lear vs. his daughters, Lear vs. nature (the storm), Lear vs. his own pride, and the cosmic conflict between order and chaos.

### In Fiction

Fiction offers the most flexibility for developing complex, multi-layered conflicts over hundreds of pages.

**Example**: In Fyodor Dostoevsky's *The Brothers Karamazov*, the conflict operates on every level — brother vs. brother, faith vs. doubt, freedom vs. responsibility, the individual vs. God. The novel's richness comes from the way these conflicts interweave and illuminate each other.

## How to Analyze Conflict

### Step 1: Identify the Central Conflict

What is the primary struggle in the work? Who or what is struggling against whom or what?

### Step 2: Determine the Type(s) of Conflict

Is it man vs. man, man vs. self, man vs. society, man vs. nature, man vs. fate, or man vs. technology? Most works combine multiple types.

### Step 3: Trace the Development

How does the conflict develop over the course of the work? Does it intensify? Does it shift from external to internal (or vice versa)? Does the character's understanding of the conflict change?

### Step 4: Analyze the Resolution

How is the conflict resolved — or is it? Some works resolve their conflicts neatly; others leave them unresolved, reflecting the complexity of real human experience.

### Step 5: Connect to Theme

What does the conflict reveal about the work's central ideas? How does the nature of the struggle express the author's vision of human experience?

## Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Identifying Conflict as Plot

"Macbeth kills the king and is killed himself" is a plot summary, not a conflict analysis. The conflict is Macbeth's ambition vs. his moral conscience, and the force of that conflict is what makes the plot meaningful.

### Mistake 2: Identifying Only One Conflict

Most works have multiple, interlocking conflicts. Identifying only the external conflict while ignoring the internal conflict (or vice versa) produces an incomplete analysis.

### Mistake 3: Assuming Conflict Must Be Resolved

Some of literature's most powerful works end without resolving their central conflicts. The unresolved conflicts in *Hamlet* or *The Great Gatsby* are part of what makes these works so enduring.

### Mistake 4: Ignoring the Stakes

Conflict is meaningless without stakes — the consequences of winning or losing. Always ask: what is at risk in this conflict? What will be gained or lost?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a story have too much conflict?

Yes. If every scene is a crisis, the reader becomes numb. The best narratives vary the intensity of conflict, building toward climaxes and providing moments of relief.

### Is conflict necessary in every story?

Yes, in some form. Even the quietest, most contemplative literary works contain conflict — often internal. A character grappling with a memory, a choice, or a loss is experiencing conflict even if no action is taking place.

### What is the difference between conflict and tension?

Conflict is the struggle between opposing forces. Tension is the feeling of anticipation or suspense that the conflict creates. Conflict is the structural element; tension is the emotional effect.

### Can conflict exist without an antagonist?

Yes. In man vs. self conflicts, the antagonist is the character's own flaw or limitation. In man vs. nature conflicts, the antagonist is the natural world. In man vs. fate conflicts, the antagonist is destiny itself.

### How does conflict relate to theme?

The nature of the conflict often expresses the theme. A story about a character struggling against injustice has themes of justice and resistance. A story about a character struggling with their own ambition has themes of power and morality.

## Summary

Conflict is the engine of narrative — the force that drives plot, reveals character, creates meaning, and engages readers. Without conflict, there is no story.

The key principles to remember:

- The six major types: **man vs. man, man vs. self, man vs. society, man vs. nature, man vs. fate, man vs. technology**
- **External conflict** opposes the character from outside; **internal conflict** opposes the character from within
- Most works operate on **multiple levels** of conflict simultaneously
- Conflict **drives plot, reveals character, creates theme, and engages the reader**
- Conflict works differently across **genres** — poetry compresses it, drama makes it immediate, fiction develops it at length
- Analyzing conflict requires identifying the **type, development, resolution**, and **thematic significance**

Mastering the analysis of conflict transforms reading from a passive reception of events into an active engagement with the struggles that give stories their power — one that reveals how literature captures the fundamental tensions of human experience.
