# Outline — Narrator in Literature

## Working Title
Narrator in Literature: Reliable, Unreliable, and Intrusive Narrators

## Research Notes / Grounding
- The narrator is the voice or presence that tells a story; the narrator is not automatically the author.
- Wayne C. Booth popularized the critical term “unreliable narrator” in *The Rhetoric of Fiction*, describing narrators whose account the implied author invites readers to question.
- Gérard Genette’s narrative theory helps separate who speaks from who sees: narration and focalization are related but not identical.
- First-person narration often feels intimate but may be limited, biased, mistaken, self-protective, or deliberately deceptive.
- Third-person narration ranges from objective camera-like reporting to omniscient commentary that enters many minds and comments on society.
- Intrusive narrators openly interrupt, judge, address the reader, explain, or shape interpretation; this is common in older fiction but also appears in metafiction.
- Good narrator analysis asks: who tells, what do they know, what do they hide, how do they sound, and what distance exists between narrator, character, author, and reader?

## Purpose
Create a long-form Literature By Edumynt guide that explains narrator as a central narrative technique, not just “first person or third person.”

## Audience
General literature readers, teachers, students, and serious beginners. Clear, in-depth, analytical, and not exam-crammy.

## Structure
1. Introduction — every story reaches us through a telling voice.
2. What Is a Narrator? — definition and distinction from author.
3. Narrator vs Author vs Speaker vs Point of View — clarify related terms.
4. Why Narrators Matter — trust, distance, voice, knowledge, theme.
5. Main Types of Narrators — first person, second person, third person limited, omniscient, objective.
6. Reliable Narrator — what reliability means and why it is never the same as neutrality.
7. Unreliable Narrator — forms of unreliability: limited knowledge, bias, self-deception, moral blindness, manipulation.
8. Intrusive Narrator — direct address, commentary, judgment, and metafictional presence.
9. Narrator and Focalization — who speaks vs who perceives.
10. Narrator and Voice — diction, tone, rhythm, social identity, personality.
11. Detailed Examples — Nick Carraway, Huck Finn, Jane Eyre, Austen’s narrator, Oedipus/choric narration, modernist interior narration.
12. Common Mistakes — equating narrator with author, trusting first person automatically, treating omniscience as objectivity.
13. How to Analyze a Narrator — practical close-reading method.
14. FAQ.
15. Conclusion.
