# Outline — Satire in Literature

## Working Title
Satire in Literature: Humor, Criticism, Irony, and Social Meaning

## Research Notes / Grounding
- Satire is a literary mode that uses humor, irony, exaggeration, parody, ridicule, and wit to criticize human folly, vice, institutions, or social conditions.
- Classical roots include Horatian satire (gentler, amused, corrective) and Juvenalian satire (harsher, morally indignant, biting). Menippean satire targets mental attitudes, ideas, and intellectual systems more than individual behavior.
- Satire depends on a norm or implied standard: it exposes a gap between what people claim and what they actually do, or between ideals and institutions.
- Satire overlaps with irony, parody, caricature, burlesque, comedy, allegory, and dystopia but is not identical to any of them.
- Major examples: Jonathan Swift’s *A Modest Proposal* and *Gulliver’s Travels*, Alexander Pope’s *The Rape of the Lock*, Jane Austen’s social satire, George Orwell’s *Animal Farm*, Voltaire’s *Candide*, Mark Twain’s social humor.
- Strong analysis should ask: What is the target? What technique is used? What values are implied? Is the tone playful, savage, despairing, reformist, or ambivalent?

## Purpose
Create a long-form guide explaining satire as a literary mode of criticism, not merely “funny writing.”

## Audience
General literature readers, students, teachers, and serious beginners. In-depth and analysis-focused.
